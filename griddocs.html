A user manual for the living grid

GridWild turns nearby nature into a playable field map.

This guide explains the GridWild idea, the user-facing features, and the core mechanics in plain language. No setup notes, no code, no machinery behind the curtain: just what the experience is for and how it feels to use.

The whole schtick

GridWild is a field game for seeing ordinary places as alive.

The central trick is simple: the world is divided into small map squares, and every square becomes a tiny expedition. A sidewalk crack, yard edge, campus lawn, alley tree, creek bank, garden bed, or forest trail can all become part of a growing biodiversity story.

1

Notice life

GridWild nudges users to look for plants, insects, fungi, birds, trees, mosses, lichens, and other life that usually fades into background scenery.

2

Map effort

The map shows what has been explored, what is still unknown, and where biodiversity records are thin, old, rich, rare, or surprising.

3

Make fieldwork social

Quests, parties, surveys, achievements, and shareable Wildlists turn observation into momentum without making the science feel sterile.

Core mechanics

The experience runs on a small set of easy ideas.

GridWild is not about memorizing menus. It is about a loop: go somewhere, reveal the grid, observe what lives there, complete focused challenges, and leave behind a richer map for yourself and others.

The Living Map

The map is the main character of GridWild. Instead of treating a place as blank space between destinations, GridWild breaks it into squares that can be discovered, revisited, compared, and cared about.

Each square can communicate a different kind of story: maybe it is full of records, maybe nobody has looked closely, maybe it has many observers, maybe one taxon group is missing, or maybe it needs a fresh visit because past records are old.

  • Use the grid as a prompt for local exploration, not just navigation.
  • Read a neighborhood by biodiversity, effort, freshness, rarity, and human activity.
  • Move from "where am I?" to "what can I discover here?"

Fog of War

Fog makes absence visible. A foggy square means the place is unknown, underexplored, or ready for a return visit. Walking through a square can temporarily reveal it; making an observation documents it more firmly.

This creates a gentle rhythm. You can scout an area by entering it, but the strongest progress comes from actually observing life. Some visited areas fade again over time, encouraging fresh attention instead of one-and-done completion.

  • Unknown squares invite first visits.
  • Surveyed squares show recent physical exploration.
  • Documented squares represent places where life has been recorded.
  • Fading territory gives quests a reason to bring you back.

Scan and Observe

The scan flow is GridWild's field notebook moment. When you find something alive, you photograph it, tune the image if needed, and prepare it as an observation. The point is to make recording nature feel immediate while still respecting the care that good observations deserve.

Anything alive counts: a common weed, a street tree, a beetle, moss on concrete, fungi after rain, a bird overhead, or a flower in a planter. GridWild's tone is intentionally generous because biodiversity begins with noticing.

  • Capture living things from the map without leaving the field flow.
  • Adjust photos so details are easier to inspect.
  • Keep drafts when an observation needs more attention later.
  • Let observations count toward map progress, quests, parties, achievements, and Wildpoints.

Ecological Lenses

Lenses change what the map emphasizes. They are interpretive modes, like putting on different field glasses. One lens might highlight species-rich hotspots, while another highlights sparse frontier squares or unusual discoveries.

The goal is to help users ask better questions. Where is biodiversity concentrated? Where are people over-sampling? Which quiet places might be hiding interesting life? Where does cultivated life meet wilder urban edges?

  • Explorer gives a balanced overview.
  • Biodiversity emphasizes species richness.
  • Frontier points toward underexplored squares.
  • Rare Finds and Treasure encourage searching beyond obvious hotspots.
  • Gardenworld and Wild Balance frame the relationship between planted and spontaneous life.

Quests

Quests give direction when the map is full of possibilities. They might ask you to photograph one plant nearby, find an insect or spider, revisit a fading square, document fungi or lichens, add a new taxon to an area, or challenge a place where another observer dominates.

A good quest is small enough to start now and meaningful enough to improve the map. Quests can stand alone or connect to a larger survey, so a casual walk can still contribute to a bigger project.

  • Daily and local prompts reduce decision fatigue.
  • Taxon-focused quests help users notice specific kinds of life.
  • Under-observed and new-taxon quests improve data coverage.
  • Revisit quests keep older knowledge from going stale.
  • Quest evidence connects discoveries back to the challenge that inspired them.

Surveys

Surveys are the organized side of GridWild. A survey can focus on a home yard, a campus, a research station, a weekend bioblitz, a park section, a route, a habitat patch, a seasonal event, or a community project.

Conceptually, a survey says: this place matters, this is the area of interest, these are the paths or zones to pay attention to, and these are the kinds of observations we hope to collect over time.

  • Create boundaries for a place or project.
  • Mark paths, dense zones, exclusions, and field assets.
  • Set a target taxon or leave the survey open to all life.
  • Keep surveys private, shared, event-based, or public depending on the project.
  • Let quests and parties attach to survey goals.

Parties

Parties are shared field sessions. They can be casual walks with friends, scheduled public events, private meetups, or focused mini-bioblitzes. The party gives the group a title, place, time window, goal, and shared sense of progress.

Party types can be broad or focused: bird walks, ant hunts, insect sweeps, fungus forays, plant surveys, or all-life bioblitzes. The important thing is that everyone is looking together, and the observations build a combined field story.

  • Start or join a group exploration session.
  • Track progress toward a shared target.
  • Connect a party to a quest or survey area.
  • Turn a field day into a report or Wildlist afterward.

Wildlists

Wildlists are curated galleries of observations. They are less like spreadsheets and more like field stories: a collection from a walk, a party, a survey, a season, a taxon group, or a personally meaningful theme.

A Wildlist can be hand-built, generated from an event, or shaped from recent discoveries. It gives users a way to present what they found, revisit the day, and share local biodiversity in a form that feels alive.

  • Collect observations into themed galleries.
  • Publish a party or survey as a readable field story.
  • Use Wildlists as personal memory, community reporting, or lightweight education.

Field Identity

GridWild gives users a field persona separate from their scientific records. You can be a naturalist, bug hunter, birder, fungus friend, urban ranger, night moth seeker, or another kind of wanderer.

Identity is playful, but it has a purpose: it helps people feel like they belong in the act of observing. Titles, profile frames, trails, companions, hats, and other cosmetic rewards make exploration feel personal without changing the value of the observations themselves.

  • Choose a display name and explorer style.
  • Equip titles and visual flourishes earned through play.
  • Express a field personality while keeping observations grounded in real nature.

Achievements, Wildpoints, Store, and Inventory

Progression gives GridWild continuity. Observations can unlock achievements for total records, unique species, unique genera, grid coverage, months observed, taxon groups, night work, and other field patterns.

Wildpoints are earned through exploration and achievement. They can be spent on identity items such as hats, titles, frames, trails, companions, and seasonal objects. The reward loop is designed to celebrate field attention, not replace it.

  • Achievements recognize habits and milestones.
  • Wildpoints give exploration a light economy.
  • The store offers cosmetic rewards and identity details.
  • Inventory keeps earned and purchased items available for your profile.

Powered by iNaturalist

GridWild is built around real biodiversity observation, and iNaturalist is the bridge from playful field activity to community science. GridWild can prepare observations for iNaturalist so records can be identified, reviewed, and contribute beyond the game layer.

The conceptual division is clean: GridWild provides the map, motivation, quests, fog, identity, parties, and local game feel. iNaturalist provides the broader observation community and biodiversity knowledge network.

  • GridWild makes field discovery inviting and local.
  • iNaturalist connects observations to identification and community review.
  • The best outcome is playful attention that becomes useful biodiversity knowledge.

The Long-Term Vision

GridWild imagines neighborhoods, campuses, parks, yards, research stations, and city blocks as living atlases that anyone can help reveal. It is not only a tracker of what has already been found; it is a guide to what might be missing.

The deeper promise is cultural. GridWild makes local biodiversity feel approachable, social, repeatable, and worth caring about. It invites users to become the kind of person who notices the small living world, then gives that noticing structure, memory, and momentum.

The field loop is the product.

Open the map. Find your square. Follow a quest or choose your own curiosity. Scan something alive. Reveal the fog. Earn progress. Share the story. Come back later and see what changed.
